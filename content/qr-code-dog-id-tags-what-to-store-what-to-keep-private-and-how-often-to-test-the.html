QR Code Dog ID Tags: What to Store, What to Keep Private and How Often to Test the Link

By the PawVigil editorial team · Updated 2026-10-18

A QR tag is only useful if the link works, the details are current and you have not shared more than a stranger needs. This guide covers what to store, what to leave out and a testing routine that takes a couple of minutes.

Start with the legal basics

A QR code is a useful extra, but it does not replace the basics. In Great Britain, a dog in a public place should wear a collar with the owner's name and address, including postcode, on the collar or on an attached tag. A phone number is strongly advised. Dogs must also be microchipped, with the owner's details kept up to date on a database. Check current rules for your nation on GOV.UK, as requirements can differ slightly, including in Northern Ireland.

The practical approach is to treat the QR code as a back-up layer. Keep an engraved tag with your name, address and phone number, and use the QR profile for extra detail that is easy to update. A QR link can fail if the phone has no signal, the code is scratched, or the service changes or ends.

What to store on the profile

Think about what a stranger needs in the first two minutes after finding your dog. Keep the profile short and easy to read on a small screen.

  • Your dog's name and a short description, such as size, colour and any distinctive markings.
  • Two phone numbers, ideally yours and a trusted second contact who knows they are listed. Ask that person first.
  • A short handling note, for example “Nervous with strangers, please do not chase, call me.”
  • A line about care needs, if relevant, such as “Has a health condition, please phone me straight away.” Keep it general. Do not list diagnoses or treatment details.
  • Microchip number, if you are comfortable including it. It helps a vet or warden confirm identity quickly, but the database record is what links it to you.
  • Vet practice name, optional, if you want a finder to have a fallback route.

If your dog is found injured, in pain, struggling to breathe or may have swallowed something harmful, the finder should contact a vet immediately. You can add a line such as “If my dog seems unwell, please take them to the nearest vet.”

What to keep private

Anyone who scans the tag can see the profile, and some tags can be photographed and scanned later. Share less than you think you need to.

  • Your full home address on the online profile. If your engraved tag already carries it, there is little reason to repeat it digitally. A town or area is enough if you want to add location.
  • Routines and absences, such as walking times, work hours, holiday dates or “home alone all day”.
  • Children's names, schools and other family details.
  • Email addresses you use for banking or other important accounts. Consider a separate address for pet admin.
  • Insurance policy numbers, database passwords and account log-ins.
  • Detailed health or medication information. Your vet holds that, and a finder cannot act on it safely.
  • Social media handles, unless you are happy for strangers to find your wider online presence.

Check the privacy settings

Many QR services let you hide certain fields, send you a message instead of showing your number, or notify you when the tag is scanned. Read the settings once, then choose what is visible. If you cannot work out what is public, scan the code on a phone that is not logged in and see what appears.

How often to test the link

Testing is quick, and it catches most problems before they matter. A sensible routine looks like this:

  1. On day one: scan the tag on your own phone, then on a different phone, ideally one on another operating system. Try it on mobile data as well as Wi-Fi.
  2. Monthly: scan it once. Confirm the page loads quickly, the phone numbers are correct and the text is readable.
  3. After any change: new phone number, new address, new contact person, new email or a replacement tag. Update the QR profile, the engraved tag if needed and the microchip database in the same sitting.
  4. Every three months: check the subscription or renewal date if the service has one, and read any notices from the provider. Make sure you still receive scan alerts.
  5. Before travel or busy periods: test again before holidays, kennel stays or events where your dog may be around unfamiliar people.

If a scan fails, do not assume it was a one-off. Clean the tag, try again in better light, and contact the provider if it keeps failing. Until it is fixed, make sure the engraved details are readable.

Check the tag and collar as well

A perfect profile does not help if the tag is lost. Each month, when you scan the code, look over the physical items:

  • Is the code still sharp, with no deep scratches, peeling or fading?
  • Is the split ring or clip secure and free from wear?
  • Is the tag a sensible size and weight for your dog, and not noisy or irritating?
  • Does the collar fit correctly? You should usually be able to fit two fingers under it, but follow advice for your dog's breed and coat.
  • Is the engraved text still readable?

If you notice skin irritation, rubbing, hair loss under the collar or a sudden change in how your dog reacts to wearing it, speak to your vet. A simple action for this week

Today, scan your dog's tag on two different phones and write down what each one shows. Remove anything that tells a stranger more than they need, add a second contact if you only have one, and set a repeating monthly reminder titled “Scan dog tag.” Then check that your microchip database details match the tag.

Frequently asked questions

Can a QR code replace an engraved ID tag in the UK?

It should not. In Great Britain, a dog in a public place should wear a collar with the owner's name and address on it or on a tag. A QR code can add to this, but it depends on a phone, signal and a working service. Check GOV.UK for the rules in your nation.

Should I put my home address on the QR profile?

Usually not. If your engraved tag already carries the legally expected details, the online profile can use phone numbers and a general area instead. This limits what a stranger or someone who photographs the code can learn.

How often should I test a dog ID QR link?

Scan it on day one on two different phones, then once a month. Test it again after any change of phone number, address or tag, and before travel. Check renewal dates every few months if the service has a subscription.

What if my dog seems unwell when someone finds them?

The finder should take the dog to a vet, especially if there is pain, injury, breathing difficulty, suspected poisoning or sudden unusual behaviour. Keep the profile to contact details and a short care note, and leave medical details to your vet.
