Senior Dog Home Safety

A Room-by-Room Home Safety Checklist for Senior Dogs with Reduced Mobility

By the PawVigil editorial team · Updated 2026-11-24

Small changes at floor level can make a house far easier for an older dog to move around. This checklist takes you through each room, with a short routine to keep it up to date.

Start by watching how your dog moves around the house

Before you buy or rearrange anything, spend a day or two watching. Note where your dog hesitates, slips, circles before lying down, avoids a doorway or waits to be lifted. Those spots are your priorities. A checklist is only useful if it matches your dog's actual routes.

Walk the house at dog height, too. Kneel down and look for slick surfaces, sharp corners, trailing cables and gaps your dog could get stuck in. Changes in mobility can have many causes, so if your dog seems to be getting stiffer, slower or reluctant to move, book a check with your vet rather than assuming it is simply age.

Hallways, doorways and floors

Hard flooring is often the biggest everyday hazard. Laminate, tiles and polished wood give little grip, especially for dogs with weak back legs or long nails.

  • Create grippy pathways. Use non-slip runners or rubber-backed mats along the routes your dog uses most, such as from the bed to the back door.
  • Secure every rug. Use proper non-slip underlay or grip tape, and check the corners regularly for curling. A sliding rug can be worse than bare floor.
  • Keep paths clear. Don't leave shoes, bags, laundry baskets or toys in the main walkway, particularly at night or if your dog has poor eyesight.
  • Check thresholds. Raised door strips and door tracks can catch a dragging paw. A small ramp or threshold wedge may help.
  • Add light. A plug-in night light in the hallway helps a dog with fading vision to move about after dark.
  • Check the paws. Fur between the pads can reduce grip, and long nails change how a dog stands. Ask your vet or groomer how often these should be tended.

Living room and sleeping areas

Senior dogs often spend most of the day resting, so the bed matters. Choose a bed with a low, easy entry point and a supportive surface that does not collapse under your dog's weight. Make sure your dog can step in without climbing over a high bolster.

  1. Place beds on a non-slip surface, not directly on slick flooring.
  2. Put a bed in each room where the household spends time. Your dog may not want to climb stairs to be near you.
  3. Keep beds away from draughts, radiators and the edge of stairs.
  4. Check beds weekly for chewed areas, split seams and damp patches, and wash covers according to the label.
  5. Think about sofas and beds. If your dog is used to jumping up, a sturdy ramp or step can reduce the strain, but introduce it gradually and supervise early use.

Check for hazards at nose and tail height as well: trailing cables, low candles, houseplants within reach and coffee tables with sharp corners. If you are unsure whether a plant is safe for dogs, check with your vet and move it out of reach in the meantime.

Kitchen, bathroom and utility areas

These rooms combine hard floors, water and household products, so they deserve extra attention.

  • Wipe up spills promptly. Water bowls can dribble when a dog has trouble drinking, so place a non-slip mat under the bowl and dry the surrounding floor.
  • Raise or adjust bowls. Some dogs find it easier to eat and drink from a slightly raised bowl, but the right height varies, so ask your vet if you are unsure.
  • Secure the bin. Use a lidded or cupboard bin so a hungry dog is not tempted by scraps or packaging.
  • Store cleaning products out of reach. Keep them in closed cupboards with child-resistant catches if your dog is nosy. Wet floors treated with cleaner should be fully dry before your dog walks on them.
  • Shut the washing machine and tumble dryer. Check inside before you start a cycle.
  • Use a non-slip mat in the bathroom if your dog is bathed or follows you in.

If you suspect your dog has swallowed or licked something harmful, contact your vet or an emergency vet straight away. Do not wait for symptoms to appear.

Stairs, steps and the garden

Stairs are a common point of risk for a dog with unsteady back legs. If your dog does not need to use them, a sturdy stair gate is the simplest option. If they do, consider stair treads with good grip and keep the stairs free of clutter.

Never rely on a gate or a ramp as a guarantee. Supervise your dog on stairs or ramps until you are confident they cope, and be ready to guide or support them if needed. If your dog needs help, ask your vet or a qualified canine physiotherapist to show you a safe way to lift or support them, as the wrong technique can hurt both of you.

Outdoor checks

  • Check the step down to the garden. A low ramp or a non-slip step can help.
  • Inspect paths for algae, wet leaves and frost, which become very slippery.
  • Keep a regular toilet spot close to the door so your dog does not have to travel far.
  • Check fences and gates daily. A dog with reduced hearing or sight may wander or get confused.
  • Provide shade, shelter and a dry place to rest, and bring your dog in when the weather is extreme.

A simple weekly routine, and when to call your vet

A short, regular check works better than an occasional big overhaul. Once a week, walk the house and garden at dog height and look at the following:

  1. Are rugs and mats still firmly in place?
  2. Are walkways free of clutter and cables?
  3. Are the bed and ramp clean, dry and undamaged?
  4. Do gates, latches and cupboards still close properly?
  5. Is your dog avoiding any area they used to use?

Keep a brief note of what you see. Changes such as slipping more often, struggling to stand, reluctance to use stairs, yelping, limping, accidents indoors, laboured breathing or sudden changes in behaviour should be taken to your vet promptly. If your dog has fallen or you suspect an injury, do not wait for the next weekly check.

Frequently asked questions

Which room should I tackle first?

Start with the route your dog uses most, usually from their bed to the door or garden. Fixing slippery floors and clutter along that route gives the biggest everyday benefit.

Are rugs a good idea on hard floors?

They can add grip, but only if they are properly secured with non-slip underlay or grip tape and checked often. A loose or curling rug can create a new trip or slip hazard.

Should my senior dog avoid stairs completely?

Not always, but many owners find a stair gate sensible if a dog is unsteady. Ask your vet for advice on your dog's needs, and supervise any stair use.

How can I tell if my dog's mobility is getting worse?

Watch for more slipping, difficulty standing, reluctance to move or changes in how they get up and lie down. Do not try to work out the cause yourself. Contact your vet, especially if you notice pain, injury or a sudden change.
