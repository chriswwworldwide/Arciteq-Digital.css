Race Day

The race is decided before the buzzer — here's your timeline.

From dinner the night before to the moment you step under the arch: what to eat, when to sleep, how to warm up, and the little tricks that keep cramps, nerves and stupid mistakes out of your race.

Paul Gillingham · Your race-day preparation guide.

A calmer start begins with a clear plan. Use this timeline as a starting point, then adapt it to your start time, format and the routine you've practised in training.

Night before

Dinner, then bed

Race day starts at the dinner table the night before. Eat a plain, high-carbohydrate dinner you've had a hundred times — rice, pasta or potato with a simple protein and nothing rich, creamy or spicy. This is not the night for the interesting restaurant.

  • DinnerCarbs forward, moderate protein, low fibre, low fat. Think chicken and rice, pasta with a light sauce, or a jacket potato. Eat early enough to digest — 7 pm, not 10.
  • HydrationDrink normally through the day and have an electrolyte drink with dinner. No alcohol — even one beer shows up in your sleep and your grip.
  • Nothing newNo new foods, new supplements or new gels after Wednesday. Race week experiments end on Friday.
  • SleepYou may not sleep brilliantly — adrenaline does that. Don't chase it. The nights that mattered were Tuesday to Friday; tonight, lying still and resting is enough. Alarm plus a backup, kit laid out.

Paul's cue

"One bad night won't cost you a race. One bad stomach will. Eat boring."

Race morning

Breakfast and supplements

Breakfast is three hours before your start — early heat means an early alarm, and that's part of the plan, not a problem.

  • 3 hours outFamiliar breakfast: oats or toast with banana and honey is the classic. Low fibre, low fat, eaten calmly. This is your last real meal — make it count.
  • 90–60 minSip an electrolyte or carb drink; don't gulp. If you always have coffee, have it now — caffeine peaks in your bloodstream around 45–60 minutes after you drink it.
  • SupplementsCarry on with anything you've used all block — creatine, electrolytes, your usual pre-race routine. Add nothing new today. If you didn't test it in training, it doesn't exist.
  • 30–15 minSmall top-up only if you know it sits well — half a banana or a few chews. Then stop eating.
60 minutes

Arrive, check, start warming up

One hour before your wave you should be inside the venue, bib on, bag dropped. This is when the clock starts working for you instead of against you.

  • Toilet first. The queue is long and everyone's nervous — get it done before you start warming up, not after.
  • Heel-lock your laces. Lace through the extra top eyelet to lock your heel into the shoe — it stops your heel lifting on the sled push and saves your toes and toenails on the runs. You've done it all block; today is not the day to skip it (Stations Prep).
  • Start the warm-up. 15–20 minutes, unhurried: easy jog, dynamic mobility, then build in stages — never go from standing to sprinting.
  • Stay warm. Keep your layer on until the last moment. Cold muscles in an air-conditioned hall undo twenty minutes of jogging.
30 minutes

Walk the Roxzone, then breathe

If the venue allows it, walk the Roxzone now — or you did it yesterday at bib collection. Either way, do it deliberately.

  • Walk the route you'll run. Count your steps between the run entrance and your first station, look at the sled lanes and carpet, find where the rowers sit and how the exit flows. On race day you don't think — you follow the map in your head, and this is how the map gets drawn.
  • Spot the gotchas. Where the wall-ball lane markers are, which direction the lunges run, where spectators stand. Surprises cost seconds and composure.
  • Box breathing. Back in the warm-up area: four counts in, hold four, out four, hold four — five or six rounds. It slows your heart rate by activating the calm side of your nervous system, and it gives adrenaline somewhere to go besides your legs. Nervous energy at 30 minutes out becomes a panic first run; breathe it down now.

Paul's cue

"Your first run should feel boring. If it feels exciting, you've already made the mistake everyone makes."

10 minutes

Run the race in your head

Ten minutes out, find a quiet corner of the pen and mentally race the whole thing — but do it physically, not just in pictures.

  • Mime the stations. As you picture each station, let your body do a small version of it: hands pulling the row stroke, driving position on the sled, the wall-ball squat and throw, the lunge step. Athletes who rehearse with movement find the first rep of each station feels familiar, not foreign.
  • Rehearse the hard parts specifically. The sled-push grind, wall balls at rep 60, the lunge walk when your legs are done. See yourself calm inside the worst moments — that's the bit that transfers.
  • Finish the picture. See the last wall ball, the walk through the finish, your name on the board. Athletes who rehearse to the end don't drift mentally in the last kilometre.
  • Final fuel. If you take a pre-race gel or caffeine gel, this is when — it'll be working as you hit the middle stations, not sitting in your stomach on run one.
Start line

Position and the first kilometre

  • Seed yourself honestly. Stand where your real pace belongs. Too far forward and you get dragged out hot; too far back and you spend two kilometres weaving.
  • Run one is a promise. Whatever it feels like — and it will feel easy — run one goes at the pace on your wrist. The race doesn't start until station three. Everyone who blew up at the wall balls felt amazing at kilometre one.
  • Eyes down, rhythm up. Don't chase the sprinters next to you. They're running a different race — literally, in doubles and relays.
During the race

Fuel, cramps and the Roxzone routine

  • Energy gel timing. Take your gel 10–15 minutes before the stations that hurt you most — for most people that's the sleds or the burpee broad jumps, with a second before the lunges or wall balls on a longer race. Take it early in a run so it's working when you arrive — never inside the station, where it sits in your throat.
  • Cramp? Mustard or pickle juice. Carry a sachet of yellow mustard or a shot of pickle juice in your shorts or belt. If a cramp twinges, take it at the next Roxzone — the vinegar is thought to trip the cramp reflex through the taste receptors in your mouth and throat, which is why it can work in under a minute when an electrolyte drink can't. Cheap insurance, weighs nothing.
  • Roxzone routine. Every transition the same: run in at effort not sprint, eyes up for your lane, breathe twice before you touch anything. Five calm seconds in the Roxzone beat twenty panicked ones.
  • Water stations. Sip, splash, move on. A cup on the back of your neck does more than a cup in your stomach at this point.

Paul's cue

"The race you planned at 10 minutes out is still the right race at kilometre five. Nothing you feel in the moment is smarter than the plan."

Quick reference

The dos and don'ts

Do

  • Eat a boring, carby dinner early
  • Bank sleep Tuesday–Friday, not just Saturday
  • Breakfast 3 hours before, familiar foods only
  • Heel-lock your laces before you leave
  • Walk the Roxzone and count your steps
  • Box-breathe at 30 minutes out
  • Mime the stations in your mental rehearsal
  • Gel 10–15 min before your hardest stations
  • Carry a mustard or pickle-juice sachet
  • Hold run-one pace on your wrist, not your feelings

Don't

  • Try a new food, gel, shoe or supplement today
  • Drink alcohol the night before
  • Chase sleep — resting awake still counts
  • Gulp water in the last hour — sip instead
  • Warm up sprinting; build in stages
  • Take your layer off early in a cold hall
  • Rehearse the race as pictures only — move with it
  • Gel inside a station — take it mid-run
  • Stand around the venue for hours beforehand
  • Follow the sprinters off the line

Race-day FAQ

What should I eat before a Hyrox?

A plain, high-carb dinner the night before — rice, pasta or potato with simple protein — then a familiar breakfast three hours before your start. Small sips of carb or electrolyte drink after that. Nothing new on race day, ever.

When should I take an energy gel?

10–15 minutes before your hardest stations — usually somewhere around the sleds or burpee broad jumps, and a second before the lunges or wall balls on longer races. Take it early in a run, not inside the station.

Does pickle juice or mustard actually help cramp?

For many athletes, yes — and fast. The vinegar is thought to interrupt the cramp reflex through taste receptors rather than by fixing electrolytes, which is why it can work inside a minute. One sachet weighs nothing; carry it.

Why walk the Roxzone beforehand?

So nothing on the course is new. Count your steps between stations, spot the sled lanes and exits, and the race runs on a map you already drew instead of one you're drawing at race pace.

What is box breathing and why before the start?

Four counts in, hold four, out four, hold four, for five or six rounds. It activates the calming side of your nervous system and lowers your heart rate — which stops pre-race adrenaline turning into a blown first kilometre.
